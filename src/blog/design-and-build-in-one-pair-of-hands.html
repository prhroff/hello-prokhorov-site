Most of my projects end with a live website, not a Figma file. I design it and I build it, and over time that has changed how I design more than anything I've read about design.

The Handoff Is Where Details Go Missing

A design file is a promise about how something will look and behave. A handoff is where that promise gets translated by someone who wasn't in the room when the decisions were made. Hover states, what happens to a heading at 390 pixels, how long an image takes to arrive: these are the things that quietly fall out.

When I build what I designed, there is no translation step. If a layout doesn't survive a long German headline, I find out on Tuesday, not three weeks after launch.

I Design Differently Because I Know the Cost

Knowing how something is built makes me more honest about what's worth building. A scroll-linked animation might take a day; a clearer pricing table might take an hour and do more for the business. I can weigh those against each other in the same conversation with the client, with real numbers.

  • Fewer one-off components, because each one is something I'll maintain.
  • Motion decided in the browser, where it can actually be judged.
  • Content tested at real lengths, not placeholder lengths.

When It's Not the Right Setup

One person has limits. A product with a large application, a backend team and a release schedule needs engineers who live in that codebase every day. There, my job is the design system and the interface, and I make the handoff as clean as I can.

For marketing sites, launch pages and early products, though, one pair of hands is usually faster, cheaper and closer to what was intended. If that sounds like your project, tell me about it.
